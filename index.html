Supply shock

Energy supply disruption.

A disruption to energy supplies drives up prices. Businesses face higher production costs, and households can afford less. Inflation rises while economic activity weakens.

Read the full briefing

An unexpected disruption to energy supply pushes up energy prices across Europe. Households face higher bills, while firms pay more to produce and transport goods. As some of these costs feed through to consumer prices, inflation rises. At the same time, households can afford less and firms scale back production, weakening economic activity. The disruption gradually fades, but its effects take time to unwind.

The reference outlook holds the annualised policy rate at 3%. Choose your own path and navigate the trade-off: bring inflation towards its target, limit weakness in activity and avoid abrupt changes in interest rates.

How the exercise works

A shock hits the economy. You announce a path for interest rates over the coming quarters. The game explores how your choices change the outlook and how well they balance the policymaker’s priorities. Three building blocks connect your decisions to the result.

01 / THE STARTING POINT

The reference outlook

What might happen as the shock unfolds? We begin with paths for inflation and economic activity, together with a reference interest-rate path.

This outlook already includes the effects of the scenario’s shock. It is the starting point against which we compare your policy choices.

02 / HOW POLICY WORKS

Policy transmission

Changing interest rates affects borrowing, spending and production, and ultimately inflation. Economic models describe how these effects build up over time.

Timing matters: a change today and a change announced for a future quarter can have different effects. We combine these responses to calculate the outlook under your announced rate path. Future decisions can influence behaviour today because households and businesses plan ahead.

03 / WHAT COUNTS AS BETTER?

The policymaker’s preferences

In this game, a loss function represents the policymaker’s priorities by combining three concerns into one penalty:

  • Headline inflation: keep the overall rise in consumer prices, including food and energy, close to the target of 2%.
  • Economic activity: limit weakness or overheating relative to what the economy can sustainably produce. We measure this with the output gap.
  • Smooth policy: avoid abrupt changes in interest rates.

Each concern has a weight, set by the controller. A higher weight gives that concern more importance in the score. These are illustrative game preferences.

Your score brings the three blocks together. We compare the penalty under your chosen path with the penalty in the reference outlook, using the same preferences. A positive score means you improved the balance; a negative score means the overall penalty increased. You can improve one outcome while making another worse—that is the policy trade-off.

Implementation details: model, calculations and scoring

The model and the counterfactual

Read the model paper · Explore COPPs

The scenario paths are constructed for teaching; they are separate from the model’s monetary-policy responses.

We use response matrices whose rows index response dates and whose columns index shock dates. Let H be the evaluation horizon and K the number of consecutive shocks at dates 0,…,K−1. The player chooses only the first K rates. We solve RK,Kε = (i0:K−1 − iref0:K−1)/4. We then add PH,Kε to reference inflation, XH,Kε to the reference output gap and 4RH,Kε to the reference rate path. Full commitment sets K = H; meeting-to-meeting mode lets the controller choose K ≤ H. The factor of 4 converts annualised rate choices into quarterly-rate units.

Only the first K consecutive shock columns are available. With K < H, later rate levels follow the response implied by those shocks and the policy rule embedded in the model. We do not fit a full H-period requested path by least squares. The 0.25-point grid applies to chosen rates; implied later rates retain full precision. A continuation below the lower bound is rejected, not clamped. All H actual rate changes, including the model-implied continuation, enter smoothing loss.

“Meeting to meeting” here means a shorter commitment window within one scenario calculation. It does not introduce new information, rolling re-optimisation or additional policy shocks in later rounds.

All calculations run in the browser. Evaluated results are not uploaded: the controller records gametags and scores separately, then updates the scenario leaderboards as a static website file.

The loss and the score

Inflation and output-gap deviations are squared, so larger misses count disproportionately more. The rate-change term includes the move from the controller’s rate before Q1 to the first chosen rate. The same inherited rate applies to the reference and the player.

Score = 100 × (1 − your loss / reference loss). If reference loss is zero, percentage improvement is undefined. The decomposition uses the total reference loss as the denominator for every contribution, so the contributions add up to the score.

GDP growth and the output gap

GDP growth combines changes in potential output and changes in the output gap. Potential output is common to the reference and the policy counterfactual. The GDP chart uses 100 × (log Yt − log Yt−4), a first-order approximation to year-on-year percentage growth, and four pre-game quarters with a zero output gap. The score always uses the output gap.
